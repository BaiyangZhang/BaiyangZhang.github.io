

Solitons first appeared in hydrodynamics when John Scott Russell observed a solitary water wave propagating down a canal in 1834 without changing its shape or speed. The non-dissipative, localized solutions of nonlinear wave equations have played an important role in optics, condensed matter physics, cosmology, and particle physics. In relativistic field theory, the simplest topological solitons arise in $(1+1)$-dimensional real scalar models whose potentials possess degenerate minima, such as the sine-Gordon and $\\phi^4$ double-well theories. A one dimensional configuration that interpolates between two different vacua at spatial boundary is known as a kink, while its higher-dimensional planar counterpart is a domain wall. Because a kink connects topologically disconnected components of the vacuum manifold, its stability is a result of topological non-triviality, hence such kinks are examples of topological solitons with nonzero topological charge. In the weak-coupling regime, the classical kink solution and its energy scales inversely with the coupling constant, making it essentially non-perturbative.   



Understanding how classical solitons lift to quantum states has been a longstanding problem since Skyrme conjectured that classical solitons correspond to quantum states with an indefinite number of meson excitations \\cite{Skyrme1958}. In perturbative quantum field theory, the vacuum sector is constructed as a Fock space of plane-wave excitations built on a translation-invariant vacuum state. However, a soliton generally connects different minima in field space, hence can not be describe by a vector in vacuum sector Hilbert space. Early works proposed that static quantum solitons are described by coherent states, see\~\\cite{Vinciarelli1972, Cornwall1974, Cahill1976, Taylor1978} and references therein. Soliton coherent states are constructed by acting on the perturbative vacuum with the unitary displacement operator, which is constructed from the canonical momentum operator smeared with the classical soliton solution. While this coherent state construction shifts the field expectation value to the soliton profile function and works well at leading order in $1+1$ dimensions, Sidney Coleman famously pointed out in his 1975 Erice lectures that coherent states fail in higher dimensions because their expectation value of the Hamiltonian density is ultraviolet divergent \\cite{Coleman1985}. Even in $1+1$ dimensions, an ordinary coherent state is neither a Hamiltonian eigenstate nor translation-invariant, hence its energy is higher than the true ground state in the kink sector\~\\cite{Evslin2019, Evslin2022}. The details of how such a state relaxes into a lower energy states remains largely unexplored. In the vacuum sector, plane waves are turned off in the ground state, whereas in the presence of a soliton, the relevant free modes are the Sturm-Liouville normal modes, including zero modes, possible discrete bound shape modes, and continuum (scattering) modes. The operators that annihilate these normal modes are related to the plane-wave annihilation operators by a Bogoliubov transformation, which is implemented by a squeeze operator. Consequently, the leading-order soliton ground state is not a simple coherent state, but a squeezed coherent state. It was shown that squeezing cures this divergence by introducing a negative energy density that cancels the ultraviolet divergence of the domain wall tension in $3+1$ dimensions \\cite{Evslin2024, Evslin2025}.



Historically, perturbative calculations in the soliton sector began with the evaluation of the one-loop correction to the kink mass by Dashen, Hasslacher, and Neveu, who summed the zero-point energies of the normal modes in a finite box \\cite{Dashen1974}. To extend soliton quantization systematically, the collective coordinate method was introduced by Gervais, Jevicki, etc.\~\\cite{Gervais1975, Gervais1976, Christ1975, Tomboulis1975, Faddeev1978}. By promoting the soliton position to a dynamical operator, the collective coordinate method maintains manifest translation invariance. However, the collective coordinate is not canonical, resulting in an infinite series of interaction terms in the Hamiltonian and modifies the path integral measure. Furthermore, traditional approaches expand the classical Hamiltonian around the kink profile before performing regularization, and then regularize the vacuum Hamiltonian and the kink Hamiltonian separately. As discovered by Rebhan and van Nieuwenhuizen \\cite{Rebhan1997}, this mismatch between the regulators of the vacuum and kink sectors leads to ambiguous and often incorrect results for the one-loop kink mass, unless ad hoc regulator matching conditions are imposed \\cite{Goldhaber2004, Weigel2019}. Linearized Soliton Perturbation Theory (LSPT) \\cite{Evslin2019, EvslinGuo2021} resolves this mismatch by regularizing and normal-ordering the defining Hamiltonian only once in the Schr\\"{o}dinger picture. From a passive perspective, both the physical state and the regularized Hamiltonian remain unchanged; one defines the shifted kink field operator , which has a vanishing expectation value in the soliton ground state at leading order, and rewrites the same regularized Hamiltonian in terms of this soliton perturbative field operator via unitary conjugation. Ultraviolet divergences in the soliton sector are removed by the exact same counterterms fixed in the vacuum sector \\cite{EvslinRoystonZhang2023}. Moreover, decomposing the kink field operator linearly into normal modes keeps the coordinates canonical, while translation invariance is restored order by order through a simple algebraic recursion relation.



While one-loop quantum corrections can be obtained in various ways such as phase-shift, spectral, or heat-kernel methods \\cite{Cahill1976, GrahamWeigel2022}, going beyond one loop is essential for both formal consistency and physical phenomenology. In the history of quantum kink mass calculations, two-loop corrections were for decades restricted to integrable models like the sine-Gordon theory or supersymmetric theories where non-renormalization theorems and the Bogomolnyi bound constrain the result. For non-integrable, non-supersymmetric theories such as the $\\phi^4$ model, determining the two-loop kink mass and its wave function corrections requires a higher-loop calculation framework. Furthermore, in the absence of a known Lehmann-Symanzik-Zimmermann (LSZ) reduction formula for the soliton sector, explicit higher-order wave function corrections are required to compute physical scattering observables\~\\cite{EvslinLiu2023}. These include inelastic meson production in kink-meson collisions\~\\cite{LiuEvslinZhang2023}, Stokes and anti-Stokes excitation of internal shape modes \\cite{EvslinLiu2023Stokes}, loop corrections to elastic scattering amplitudes off reflectionless kinks \\cite{EvslinLiu2024}, and the radiative decay lifetimes of unstable excitations and oscillons\~\\cite{may-long-lived}.



As calculations in LSPT are pushed to two loops and higher orders in the coupling $g = \\sqrt{\\lambda}$, or extended to beyond $1+1$ dimensions where ultraviolet renormalization in the Schr\\"{o}dinger picture is more complex, the number of operator contractions and multi-meson Fock space components grows rapidly. An efficient and systematic calculation tool is therefore needed to organize the perturbative expansion. In this paper, building on the passive formulation of LSPT, we introduce an intuitive, easy-to-track diagrammatic method designed for Schr\\"{o}dinger-picture state and mass perturbation theory. By decomposing each interaction Hamiltonian $\\mathcal{H}\_ {\\text{int}}$ according to the net number of mesons created or annihilated, we represent operator actions as vertices and multi-meson Fock space projections as vertical slices across which the coupling order increases from right to left. We establish complete diagrammatic rules for vertex weights, energy denominators, and composite lower-order state vertices, and apply this method to compute renormalized vacuum and one-meson wave function corrections up to $\\mathcal{O}(g^3)$, demonstrating the explicit cancellation of infrared and ultraviolet divergences and providing a streamlined framework for higher-order soliton mass and wave function corrections.
